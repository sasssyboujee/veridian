\begin{abstract}
South Africa's electricity distribution system is characterised by
chronic load-shedding, capital-intensive grid infrastructure, and a
structural inability to extend reliable service to the estimated
14.7~million households that lack dependable access. Distributed solar
photovoltaic (PV) generation offers a technically viable alternative;
however, retail financing constraints and the absence of institutional
compliance mechanisms prevent mass adoption.

This paper presents \emph{Veridian}, a decentralized physical
infrastructure network (DePIN) protocol that tokenizes cash-flowing solar
installations in South Africa under an Equipment-as-a-Service (EaaS)
model. The protocol combines three mechanisms: (i) hardware-attested
telemetry from TPM~2.0 solar meters that produce cryptographically signed
generation records, (ii) ERC-3643 compliant fractional security tokens
whose transfers are gated by on-chain identity verification, and (iii)
deterministic USDC yield settlement executed on Optimism Sepolia via a
3-of-5 multisignature escrow. A strict revenue waterfall allocates
proceeds as follows: 75\% to token holders, 8\% to operations and
maintenance, 7\% to an insurance reserve, 5\% to a pool expansion fund,
and 5\% to platform operations. An end-to-end simulation on a representative
450~W panel at 5.5 sun-hours per day produces a verifiable net yield of
20.7\% per annum, which the smart contract suite distributes automatically
to verified fractional owners without custodial intermediation. The
architecture is evaluated for its ability to disintermediate Eskom's
centralized grid monopoly, reduce household energy costs by at least
10\% relative to regulated tariffs, and provide retail investors with
compliant, yield-bearing exposure to physical solar infrastructure.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =============================================================================

South Africa's electricity sector presents a confluence of structural
failure and latent opportunity that is well-suited to blockchain-enabled
infrastructure finance. Eskom Holdings SOC Ltd., the vertically integrated
state-owned utility, controls the overwhelming majority of generation,
transmission, and distribution capacity. Since 2007, Eskom has
implemented rolling load-shedding protocols, reaching Stage~6 curtailment
in 2022 and 2023, with cumulative lost economic output estimated by the
South African Reserve Bank at over R900~billion \cite{nersa2023}. The
World Bank estimates that 14.7~million South Africans lack reliable
electricity access, with peri-urban and rural communities disproportionately
affected \cite{worldbank2023}.

The solar resource across South Africa averages between 4.5 and
6.5~kWh~m$^{-2}$~day$^{-1}$ of Global Horizontal Irradiance (GHI),
placing the country among the highest-irradiance jurisdictions globally
\cite{saws2023solar,irena2022south_africa}. This resource is geographically
distributed and does not require transmission infrastructure to reach
end-users when harnessed at the household or community scale. Rooftop and
ground-mounted solar PV systems of 450~W to 10~kW capacity are technically
adequate to offset the average South African household consumption of
approximately 897~kWh per month \cite{eskom2024tariff}.

Despite favorable resource conditions, retail solar adoption is constrained
by two structural barriers. First, the upfront capital cost of a
complete 450~W panel system (approximately R6,750 in 2024) is prohibitive
for the median South African household income. Second, retail investors
seeking exposure to distributed solar infrastructure have no regulated,
liquid, and transparent mechanism to participate without deploying
project-scale capital.

The Equipment-as-a-Service (EaaS) model addresses the first barrier by
converting the capital expenditure of solar acquisition into a per-unit
consumption fee paid by the household, analogous to a utility subscription.
Prior work on peer-to-peer energy trading \cite{pop2018peer,andoni2019blockchain}
has demonstrated the technical feasibility of blockchain-mediated energy
markets; however, these systems typically address bilateral settlement
between grid-connected prosumers rather than providing a compliant
fractional financing layer for the physical equipment itself.

Decentralized Physical Infrastructure Network (DePIN) protocols represent
a recent architectural pattern that coordinates the deployment and
operation of physical infrastructure through on-chain incentive structures
\cite{zhang2022depin}. Applied to energy infrastructure, a DePIN protocol
can theoretically align the economic interests of equipment investors,
field operators, and energy consumers without a central coordinating
entity. Two specific research gaps remain open in this domain. First,
existing DePIN energy protocols (e.g., Power Ledger, SolarCoin) do not
incorporate hardware-level attestation of physical generation data;
they rely on software-reported metrics or grid-metered totals, creating
an unresolved oracle problem at the physical layer. Second, no published
implementation combines compliance-gated fractional securities (as
required for regulated investment products) with automated yield
settlement in a developing-market context where centralized grid
reliability is structurally inadequate.

This paper addresses both gaps through two primary contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{End-to-end trust chain from physical attestation to
    regulated settlement.} The Veridian protocol specifies a verifiable
    pipeline in which TPM~2.0 hardware-signed telemetry propagates
    through an off-chain yield engine to an ERC-3643 compliant on-chain
    distribution contract. The architectural contribution is the formal
    linkage between physical hardware attestation and compliance-gated
    token settlement, which no existing DePIN protocol provides.
  \item \textbf{Oracle-referenced governance constraint for consumer
    protection.} The protocol introduces an on-chain price cap mechanism
    ($P_{\text{proposed}} \leq 0.90 \times P_{\text{grid}}$) that
    prevents investor-captured DAOs from imposing extractive pricing on
    energy consumers. This is a novel governance design contribution
    that addresses an unsolved incentive alignment problem in DePIN
    protocol governance.
\end{enumerate}

Supporting these contributions, the paper provides: (a) a parameterized
economic model of a 450~W South African solar installation producing a
base-case net yield of 20.7\% per annum with sensitivity analysis across
irradiance and degradation scenarios, (b) a formal definition of the
75/8/7/5/5 revenue waterfall and 3-of-5 multisignature escrow mechanics,
and (c) a gas cost analysis comparing push and pull distribution patterns
on Optimism.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section~\ref{sec:background}
reviews related work on blockchain-based energy systems and DePIN
architectures. Section~\ref{sec:architecture} describes the three-layer
Veridian protocol architecture. Section~\ref{sec:economic} presents the
economic model and revenue waterfall. Section~\ref{sec:implementation}
describes the implementation and simulation results.
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} evaluates the system against security,
scalability, and compliance requirements. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}
concludes.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Blockchain-Based Energy Markets}

Andoni et al.\ \cite{andoni2019blockchain} provide a systematic review of
blockchain applications in the energy sector, identifying peer-to-peer
energy trading, grid management, and carbon credit tokenization as the
dominant use-cases. Power Ledger, one of the earliest production
deployments, enables bilateral energy trading between grid-connected
prosumers using a dual-token model. However, Power Ledger and similar
systems presuppose functioning grid interconnection and do not address
the capital formation problem for equipment acquisition.

Pop et al.\ \cite{pop2018peer} evaluate Ethereum-based peer-to-peer energy
trading in microgrid environments and identify settlement finality and gas
cost as primary constraints for high-frequency micro-transactions. This
observation motivates the use of a Layer-2 execution environment in
Veridian, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:architecture}.

\subsection{Decentralized Physical Infrastructure Networks}

Zhang et al.\ \cite{zhang2022depin} define DePIN protocols as systems that
coordinate the deployment of physical hardware through on-chain token
incentives, where hardware owners contribute capacity in exchange for
protocol-issued rewards. The key design challenge identified in this work
is the oracle problem: verifying that physical capacity was actually
deployed and utilized without introducing centralized attestation
intermediaries. Veridian addresses this challenge through hardware-level
attestation rather than software-level oracle aggregation.

\subsection{Real-World Asset Tokenization}

Massaro et al.\ \cite{massaro2023rwa} review the regulatory architecture
of real-world asset (RWA) tokenization, finding that the principal
barriers to adoption are (i) the enforcement of investor eligibility
constraints at the token transfer layer and (ii) the linkage between
on-chain token state and off-chain legal title. The ERC-3643 standard
\cite{erc3643} directly addresses the first barrier through its
compliance module and identity registry architecture. The Special Purpose
Vehicle (SPV) structure addresses the second.

\subsection{IoT Data Integrity and Hardware Attestation}

Boudguiga et al.\ \cite{boudguiga2017iot} demonstrate that blockchain
anchoring of IoT device firmware updates provides a tamper-evident audit
trail that is computationally infeasible to forge without access to the
device's private signing key. The Trusted Computing Group's TPM~2.0
specification \cite{tpm20spec} extends this principle to a standardized
hardware security module that can be embedded in field devices to produce
cryptographically signed operational attestations. In Veridian, this
mechanism is applied to solar generation meters to produce signed
kWh telemetry that is verifiable without trusting the metering hardware
vendor.

\section{Protocol Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}
% =============================================================================

The Veridian protocol is organized into three layers: the hardware
attestation layer, the off-chain oracle and data layer, and the on-chain
execution layer. Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture} illustrates the
interaction sequence across these layers.

\begin{figure}[t]

\textbf{[On-Chain Layer: Optimism Sepolia]}\\

\label{fig:architecture}

\subsection{Hardware Attestation Layer}

Each solar installation is provisioned with a dedicated metering unit
containing a TPM~2.0 security module or a software-simulated equivalent
operating on the SECP256R1 elliptic curve. During installation, the
administrator registers the meter's public key on the off-chain backend
through a cryptographic handshake that establishes the Hardware Root of
Trust (HRoT).

At regular intervals, the meter produces a telemetry payload containing
the cumulative energy generated in kWh, a Unix timestamp, and the meter
identifier. This payload is signed using the TPM's internal ECDSA private
key, which is hardware-protected and cannot be extracted. The signed
JSON object is transmitted to the FastAPI backend:

\begin{equation}
\sigma = \text{ECDSA}_{\text{SECP256R1}}(k_{\text{priv}},\;
  H(\text{kWh} \| \text{ts} \| \text{id}))
  \label{eq:ecdsa}
\end{equation}

where $k_{\text{priv}}$ is the TPM-resident private key, $H(\cdot)$ is
a SHA-256 hash function, and $\text{ts}$ is the Unix timestamp.

The backend verifies $\sigma$ against the registered public key $k_{\text{pub}}$.
Any tampered or replayed payload fails verification and is rejected before
database insertion. This mechanism ensures that the yield calculation
is grounded in cryptographically verifiable physical generation records
rather than operator-supplied data.

A production guard on the variance between hardware-signed utilization
records and physical maintenance audit findings is set at a maximum of
5\%. If discrepancy exceeds this threshold, the operator's staked deposit
is subject to slashing via the escrow contract.

\subsection{Off-Chain Oracle and Yield Engine}

The oracle layer consists of a FastAPI application backed by a PostgreSQL
database. The database schema maintains four primary relations:
\texttt{assets} (token address, SPV status, jurisdiction), \texttt{billing\_cycles}
(computation periods), \texttt{telemetry\_logs} (verified generation history),
and \texttt{yield\_calculations} (gross and net yield breakdowns).

The yield engine computes gross revenue per billing cycle as:

\begin{equation}
G = q \times p
\label{eq:yield}
\end{equation}

where $q$ is the total energy consumed (kWh) by the lessee household
over the billing cycle, and $p$ is the contracted EaaS per-unit rate
(ZAR/kWh). The per-unit rate is constrained on-chain by the price cap
mechanism described in Section~\ref{sec:pricecap}. The engine then
applies the 75/8/7/5/5 waterfall to produce per-tranche allocations
and formats the net investor yield as an ABI-encoded payload denominated
in USDC-wei (18 decimal precision) for consumption by the
\texttt{YieldDistributor} contract.

The oracle endpoint \texttt{/yields/oracle/\{asset\_id\}} is authenticated
via a rotation-based API key linked to the Chainlink Functions
subscription. This endpoint returns a deterministic payload that the
Chainlink Forwarder submits to the on-chain \texttt{onReport()} function.

\subsection{On-Chain Execution Layer (Optimism Sepolia)}

Optimism Sepolia was selected as the execution environment for three
reasons. First, the Optimistic Rollup architecture inherits the
security properties of Ethereum Layer~1 through fraud proofs, while
reducing transaction fees by approximately two orders of magnitude.
Second, the near-zero cost of micro-transactions on Optimism makes
monthly yield distributions to large numbers of fractional token holders
economically viable; the same distribution executed on Ethereum mainnet
would consume yields for low-value holdings. Third, Optimism's EVM
equivalence ensures full compatibility with the existing ERC-3643
contract suite and the Foundry toolchain used to deploy and test the
contracts \cite{optimism2023}.

The on-chain contract suite consists of four primary components:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{RWAToken.sol}: An ERC-3643 compliant token contract
    that gates all transfers through a \texttt{canTransfer} pre-check
    against the \texttt{IdentityRegistry}.
  \item \textbf{IdentityRegistry.sol}: An on-chain whitelist that maps
    investor wallet addresses to verified ONCHAINID claims \cite{onchainid}.
  \item \textbf{MultiSigEscrow.sol}: A 3-of-5 threshold multisignature
    contract that holds investor capital during the offering period.
  \item \textbf{YieldDistributor.sol}: A Chainlink-triggered contract
    that enforces the revenue waterfall and executes USDC distributions.
\end{enumerate}

The 75\% investor tranche is further modulated by a time-lock staking
mechanism. Token holders who lock their ERC-3643 tokens in the
\texttt{RWAVault} contract receive the full 75\% allocation; liquid
(unstaked) holders receive 60\%, with the 15\% differential returned to
the depreciation reserve. This design encourages long-term capital
commitment and reduces secondary market volatility.

The 7\% insurance reserve is critical to the asset-backed nature of the
protocol. In the event of panel failure, fire, or theft, insurance
proceeds are routed through the SPV, converted to USDC, and returned to
token holders via the \texttt{YieldDistributor}'s liquidation path.
The 5\% expansion fund accumulates on-chain until a governance vote
approves the acquisition of a new solar array, at which point it is
routed to the \texttt{MultiSigEscrow} for a new capital formation cycle.

\subsection{The 3-of-5 Multisignature Escrow Mechanics}
\label{sec:multisig}

During the initial offering period, investor USDC capital is held in the
\texttt{MultiSigEscrow} contract. Capital release to the equipment
seller requires the cryptographic confirmation of at least 3 out of 5
designated signers. The five signer roles are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Platform Operator}: Veridian protocol administrator.
  \item \textbf{Solar Operator}: SunFix Logistics field representative.
  \item \textbf{SPV Representative}: Director of the asset-holding SPV entity.
  \item \textbf{Legal Representative}: Licensed conveyancer or
    commercial attorney confirming title transfer.
  \item \textbf{Independent Verifier}: An auditor or regulatory
    observer confirming physical installation.
\end{enumerate}

The 3-of-5 threshold is chosen to balance operational efficiency (a single
party failure does not block the protocol) against security (any two
parties acting collusively cannot release capital unilaterally). The
contract is deployed with all five signer addresses registered at
instantiation time; signer rotation requires a full 5-of-5 vote, making
signer substitution an extraordinary governance action.

Capital is released from escrow in a single transaction only after the
SPV Representative and Legal Representative have both signed, confirming
off-chain legal title transfer. This two-role requirement ensures that
the on-chain capital release is causally linked to the off-chain legal
event, providing token holders with enforceable asset-backed collateral.

\subsection{Oracle-Referenced Household Price Cap}
\label{sec:pricecap}

To prevent investor governance from imposing extractive EaaS rates on
lessee households, the protocol enforces a strict price cap constraint
directly in the smart contract execution layer. The \texttt{PricingController}
contract queries a Chainlink Price Feed that tracks the current
regulated Eskom residential tariff ($P_{\text{grid}}$) and enforces:

\begin{equation}
P_{\text{proposed}} \leq 0.90 \times P_{\text{grid}}
\label{eq:pricecap}
\end{equation}

Any DAO governance proposal that sets the EaaS rate above this ceiling
is automatically reverted by the contract. This structural constraint
guarantees that lessee households always pay at least 10\% below the
Eskom regulated tariff, preserving the economic incentive for households
to adopt the EaaS model and protecting the protocol from the reputational
and default risk of predatory pricing.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Smart Contract Suite}

The Veridian smart contract suite is implemented in Solidity~0.8.x and
deployed using the Foundry toolchain on Optimism Sepolia
(Chain ID~11155420). The full contract source is available in the
project repository at \url{https://github.com/sasssyboujee/veridian}.
The four primary contracts are:

\textbf{RWAToken.sol} inherits from \texttt{ERC-3643} and
\texttt{ERC20Votes}. Every \texttt{transfer} and \texttt{transferFrom}
call is intercepted by the \texttt{canTransfer} pre-check, which queries
the \texttt{IdentityRegistry} via the \texttt{isVerified(address)}
function. Transfers to non-whitelisted addresses are automatically
reverted. The contract exposes \texttt{forcedTransfer} and
\texttt{setAddressFrozen} functions accessible only to the designated
Agent role, enabling compliance-mandated asset recovery without
disrupting protocol state.

\textbf{IdentityRegistry.sol} maintains the triple registry structure
required by ERC-3643: the \texttt{ClaimTopicsRegistry} (defining required
claim types, e.g., KYC clearance, AML status, jurisdiction), the
\texttt{TrustedIssuersRegistry} (enumerating authorized KYC providers),
and the address-to-identity mapping. In the current testnet deployment,
the \texttt{AutoKYC} contract acts as a mock claim issuer that directly
injects an approved identity claim upon wallet submission. Production
deployment requires replacement with a regulated KYC provider integration.

\textbf{MultiSigEscrow.sol} implements the 3-of-5 threshold logic
described in Section~\ref{sec:multisig}. Investor USDC deposits are
accumulated in the contract's token balance. A \texttt{confirmRelease}
function increments a per-signer confirmation bitmap; when the confirmation
count reaches 3, the \texttt{executeRelease} function becomes callable
and transfers the full balance to the registered equipment seller address.

\textbf{YieldDistributor.sol} implements the waterfall logic. The
\texttt{onReport} function, accessible only to the registered Chainlink
\texttt{KeystoneForwarder} address, receives the ABI-encoded oracle
payload containing the \texttt{net\_yield\_wei} scalar and a
\texttt{token\_snapshot} mapping of holder addresses to balances. The
contract iterates over the snapshot, computing each holder's proportional
allocation as:

\begin{equation}
Y_i = \frac{b_i}{B_{\text{total}}} \times 0.75 \times G_{\text{usdc}}
\label{eq:holderyield}
\end{equation}

where $b_i$ is the holder's token balance, $B_{\text{total}}$ is the
total supply, and $G_{\text{usdc}}$ is the gross yield denominated in
USDC-wei. The remaining 25\% is partitioned into the four protocol
tranches (8\%, 7\%, 5\%, 5\%) and transferred to their respective
registered wallet addresses in the same transaction.

\subsection{Token Issuance and ERC-3643 Compliance Flow}

The issuance of fractional solar tokens follows a six-step compliance
sequence. First, the investor submits their wallet address to the KYC
portal. Second, the \texttt{AutoKYC} claim issuer (or, in production, a
regulated provider) verifies the investor's identity and issues a signed
claim bound to their ONCHAINID. Third, the \texttt{IdentityRegistry}
whitelists the investor's address. Fourth, the investor deposits USDC
into the \texttt{MultiSigEscrow}. Fifth, upon 3-of-5 confirmation and
legal title transfer, the escrow releases capital and the
\texttt{RWAFactory} deploys a new \texttt{RWAToken} instance and mints
fractional tokens proportional to each investor's contribution. Sixth,
the \texttt{RWAFactory} also deploys a dedicated \texttt{RWAGovernor}
DAO contract for the asset, granting token holders voting power
proportional to their fractional ownership.

\subsection{Gas Cost and Throughput Evaluation}
\label{sec:gascost}

The \texttt{YieldDistributor} contract uses a push-based distribution
pattern that iterates over all token holders in a single transaction.
This design has $O(n)$ gas complexity, where $n$ is the number of
holders. On Optimism, each USDC transfer within the loop consumes
approximately 26,000~gas (21,000 base + 5,000 for ERC-20 storage
writes). The total gas cost per distribution cycle is therefore:

\begin{equation}
C_{\text{push}}(n) = C_{\text{base}} + n \times C_{\text{transfer}}
\label{eq:gascost}
\end{equation}

where $C_{\text{base}} \approx 50{,}000$ gas (function dispatch,
oracle verification, waterfall split) and
$C_{\text{transfer}} \approx 26{,}000$ gas per holder.
Table~\ref{tab:gascost} presents the estimated gas consumption
and USD cost at an Optimism L2 gas price of 0.001~Gwei.

transaction. Second, for holders with very small fractional positions,

A Merkle-proof pull distribution pattern provides an $O(1)$ alternative.
In this model, the contract administrator publishes a Merkle root of
the yield allocation tree, and each holder independently submits a
proof to claim their yield. The gas cost per claim is approximately
50,000~gas regardless of the total holder count, with the trade-off
that unclaimed yields accumulate until the holder initiates a claim
transaction. The pull pattern is recommended for deployments exceeding
1,000 holders and is planned for future protocol versions.

\subsection{Security Analysis}

The protocol defines five quantitative circuit-breaker thresholds.
Breach of any threshold triggers automated protective action:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Telemetry Variance} ($\leq$5\%): Discrepancy between
    hardware-signed generation data and physical audit findings triggers
    operator stake slashing.
  \item \textbf{Secondary Market Liquidity Ratio} ($\geq$15\% of market
    cap within 30 days): Failure indicates inadequate secondary market
    depth; automated circuit breaker halts new token issuance.
  \item \textbf{Governance Quorum Floor} ($\geq$10\% of circulating supply):
    Failure on critical votes triggers automatic fallback to the
    Maintenance Council, preventing operational paralysis.
  \item \textbf{Price Cap Compliance}: Any proposed rate exceeding
    $0.90 \times P_{\text{grid}}$ (equation \ref{eq:pricecap}) is
    automatically reverted by the \texttt{PricingController}.
  \item \textbf{Yield-to-CapEx Accumulation}: The expansion fund requiring
    more than 24 months to accumulate a 20\% down payment on a
    new mid-tier array signals stalled network growth and triggers
    a governance review.
\end{enumerate}

The hardware attestation layer provides a strong security guarantee
for the telemetry data. Because the TPM's private key is hardware-resident
and cannot be exported, an adversary seeking to inflate reported generation
must either compromise the physical meter hardware or conduct a
man-in-the-middle attack between the meter and the backend API. The
ECDSA verification in the backend (equation \ref{eq:ecdsa}) and the
5\% variance threshold provide complementary defenses against these
attack vectors.

\subsection{Trust Model and Limitations}
\label{sec:trustmodel}

The Veridian prototype operates under a \emph{two-tier trust model}
that distinguishes between design-level security guarantees and the
current evaluation-level trust assumptions. This distinction is critical
for an accurate assessment of the protocol's maturity.

\textbf{Tier~1: Hardware Attestation (Design-level guarantee; currently
simulated).} The protocol design specifies that telemetry integrity is
anchored to a TPM~2.0 hardware root of trust whose private key is
non-exportable. Under physical deployment, an adversary seeking to forge
generation data must compromise the physical meter hardware itself,
reducing the threat surface to physical tampering rather than software
exploitation. However, in the current prototype, the TPM is simulated
using software-generated SECP256R1 keys. The signing key is therefore
extractable, and the hardware attestation guarantee is \emph{unverifiable}
at the evaluation level. The development bypass in
\texttt{tpm\_verify.py} further weakens this tier by permitting
unverified payloads. The threat surface reduction that physical TPM
deployment would achieve is bounded by the TPM~2.0 specification's
key non-exportability guarantee \cite{tpm20spec}; the current simulation
validates the protocol flow and payload format, but not the
tamper-resistance property.

\textbf{Tier~2: Oracle Delivery (Decentralized consensus guarantee;
currently centralized).} The protocol design specifies yield distribution
triggered by a Chainlink Functions decentralized oracle network, where
multiple independent nodes reach consensus on the yield payload before
submission to the \texttt{YieldDistributor}. In the current prototype,
this delivery is simulated by a single FastAPI backend making a direct
contract call. This means the current system is architecturally
equivalent to a centralized platform distributing funds via an API
call. The ``deterministic settlement'' claim therefore applies to the
\emph{contract-level execution logic} (which enforces the waterfall
regardless of the caller) but not to the \emph{trigger mechanism},
which currently lacks decentralized consensus.

Both tiers must be independently evaluated when assessing the
protocol's security posture. The current prototype operates at the
weakest level of both tiers: simulated hardware attestation and
centralized oracle delivery.

In addition to the trust model limitations, two further components
require production-grade replacement:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{AutoKYC (Mocked)}: The \texttt{AutoKYC} claim issuer
    bypasses identity verification. Production deployment requires
    integration with a regulated KYC provider (e.g., Onfido, Jumio)
    connected to the FSCA's AML/CFT reporting framework.
  \item \textbf{Midnight Network zk-KYC (Proposed)}: The integration of
    Midnight Network zero-knowledge proofs for selective identity
    disclosure is architecturally specified but not implemented. This
    component would enable investors to prove KYC clearance without
    exposing personal data to the public ledger.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Structural Disintermediation of Grid Monopoly}

The Veridian protocol achieves grid-monopoly disintermediation through
a combination of physical infrastructure bypass and financial
democratization, rather than through regulatory confrontation. By
providing households with solar energy via the EaaS model at a rate
contractually bounded below the Eskom regulated tariff (equation
\ref{eq:pricecap}), the protocol reduces the household's dependence on
grid supply without requiring grid disconnection. As panel fleets scale,
the aggregate distributed generation capacity represents a meaningful
reduction in peak demand that Eskom would otherwise need to supply
through thermal generation.

From the investor perspective, the protocol provides access to an asset
class (physical solar infrastructure) that has historically been
accessible only to utilities, pension funds, and infrastructure
private equity. Fractional tokenization at the single-panel level (R6,750
capital cost per unit) reduces the minimum investment to a retail
scale. The ERC-3643 compliance layer ensures that this democratization
does not come at the cost of regulatory compliance or investor
protection.

\subsection{Scalability Pathway}

The current simulation operates at a single-panel granularity.
Fleet-scale deployment would aggregate multiple panels into a single SPV
and issue tokens representing a proportional share of the fleet's
combined generation. The yield engine's \texttt{billing\_cycles} schema
supports multi-asset aggregation natively; the primary technical barrier
to scale is the cost of on-chain token snapshot iteration in the
\texttt{YieldDistributor}. At large holder counts, the gas cost of the
push-distribution loop could become prohibitive even on Optimism.
This motivates a future migration to a Merkle-proof-based pull
distribution model, where holders claim their yields by submitting a
proof rather than receiving an automatic airdrop.

\subsection{Regulatory Pathway for South Africa}

The SPV and token structure described in this paper constitute a
\emph{design-level legal assumption} that has not been validated by
a formal legal opinion. The following analysis identifies the relevant
South African legislation and the specific licensing requirements that
would apply.

Under the Financial Markets Act 19 of 2012, instruments that offer
profit participation derived from a third party's operational efforts
are classified as securities. The Veridian EaaS tokens, which provide
yield derived from SunFix Logistics' operation of the solar panels,
would likely satisfy this classification. Additionally, the Collective
Investment Schemes Control Act 45 of 2002 (CIS Act) may apply if the
FSCA determines that pooled investor capital managed by an SPV
constitutes a collective investment scheme, which would require a
Section~5 license from the FSCA.

The SPV itself would be incorporated under the Companies Act 71 of
2008 as a private company (Pty.~Ltd.) with the singular purpose of
holding legal title to the solar panel fleet. The ERC-3643 standard's
mandatory KYC/AML enforcement and the \texttt{IdentityRegistry}
provide the on-chain compliance infrastructure that the FSCA would
require for a retail investment product.

However, three off-chain legal prerequisites remain unresolved:
(i) formal FSCA registration as a CIS, requiring a licensed management
company and approved trust deed; (ii) a legal opinion confirming that
the ERC-3643 token transfer constitutes a valid transfer of beneficial
interest under South African law; and (iii) compliance with the
Financial Intelligence Centre Act (FICA) for customer due diligence.
These off-chain legal structures are outside the scope of the current
technical prototype and represent the primary pathway dependency for
production deployment. No formal legal opinion has been obtained, and
the regulatory analysis presented here should not be construed as
legal advice.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =============================================================================

This paper has presented Veridian, a DePIN protocol for hardware-attested
fractional financing of solar infrastructure in South Africa. The
protocol makes two primary contributions to the DePIN and decentralized
energy systems literature.

At the \emph{design level}, the protocol specifies an end-to-end trust
chain from TPM~2.0 hardware-signed telemetry through an ERC-3643
compliance-gated token layer to deterministic USDC yield settlement on
Optimism, governed by the 75/8/7/5/5 revenue waterfall and secured by
a 3-of-5 multisignature escrow. The oracle-referenced price cap
mechanism ($P_{\text{proposed}} \leq 0.90 \times P_{\text{grid}}$)
represents a novel governance constraint that structurally prevents
investor-captured DAOs from extracting surplus from energy consumers.

At the \emph{evaluation level}, the current prototype operates under
a two-tier trust model (Section~\ref{sec:trustmodel}): hardware
attestation is simulated with software keys, and oracle delivery is
centralized. The end-to-end simulation validates the economic model and
protocol flow: a 450~W panel produces a base-case investor yield of
20.7\% per annum (18.7\% under realistic degradation and inverter loss
assumptions per Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}), with seasonal generation
bounded between 49.5~kWh in June and 101.5~kWh in January. The gas
cost analysis (Section~\ref{sec:gascost}) confirms that push-based
yield distribution is viable for up to approximately 1,150 holders on
Optimism before reaching the block gas limit, motivating a Merkle-proof
pull pattern for larger deployments.

The path from prototype to production requires resolving four
dependencies: (i) physical TPM~2.0 provisioning in field metering
hardware, (ii) regulated KYC provider integration replacing the mocked
AutoKYC, (iii) Chainlink Functions mainnet subscription for decentralized
oracle delivery, and (iv) formal FSCA registration under the CIS Act
(Section~45 of 2002) and Financial Markets Act (19 of 2012). Each
dependency is independently addressable and does not require
architectural changes to the protocol design.
